Full contents: chapters and sections

In short. The first code of Joseon, the Gyeongje yukjeon (經濟六典), was printed in 1397; the Gyeongguk daejeon (經國大典) carries a preface of 1469 and took effect from the first month of 1485. No text of the Gyeongje yukjeon survives, so this chapter measures only what can be counted. In the Veritable Records, 經濟六典 is named most often under Taejong (28 articles), and 經國大典 first appears under Sejo. The six jeon of the Gyeongguk daejeon hold 68,775 characters; the Military Code is the largest.

What this chapter covers#

Chapter 1 is built differently from the others. From Chapter 2 on, the site counts the text of the books, but the Gyeongje yukjeon and the later supplements to it (續六典) do not survive. This chapter therefore gives dates, counts of mentions in the Veritable Records, and the size of the Gyeongguk daejeon that closed the period. If the text can be recovered (a candidate for a later version), the way the provisions of the Gyeongje yukjeon line passed into the Gyeongguk daejeon can be measured as in Chapter 6.

The year of the preface (1469) and the year the code took effect (1485) differ. Revision went on after 1469; in the fifteenth year of Seongjong the Veritable Records carry several reports of the collation office (勘校廳) reconsidering provisions of the Daejeon. Since 1.0 this site places the Gyeongguk daejeon at 1485, the year it took effect.

In the Veritable Records#

經濟六典 is named most under Taejong (28 articles) and Sejong (20); 經國大典 first appears under Sejo (24). Under Seongjong both names appear (12 and 24). A count of articles does not measure how much a code was used, and articles announcing a compilation are not separated from articles quoting a provision (see Codes in the Sillok).

The size of the Gyeongguk daejeon#

Terms — the six jeon (六典): the six parts into which the codes and the collections of instructions divide their text: Personnel (吏典), Revenue (戶典), Rites (禮典), Military (兵典), Punishments, i.e. the Penal Code (刑典), and Works (工典). Jomok (條目): a headed section under a jeon. Provision: a single rule under a jomok (where one ends differs by book; see Method). Sugyo (受敎): a royal instruction.

The text of the Gyeongguk daejeon in the Korean History Database of the National Institute of Korean History (NIKH) was counted. The six jeon hold 68,775 characters and 1,022 provisions (last TOC items cut again at ○). The Military Code (18,293 characters) is the largest, followed by Personnel and Rites. The count follows the same rules as those for the Sok daejeon in Chapter 4 and the Daejeon hoetong in Chapter 6; the sizes of the compilations in Chapter 3 (open data) are counted over a different span and are not compared directly.

This text is the NIKH collated edition. Its last page carries the colophon 「辛丑 六月 日 芸閣鑄字重印」 (reprinted in movable type by the Office of Publications in the sixth month of a sinchuk year), so it cannot be taken as the first printing of 1485 as it stood. Some of the NIKH collation notes correct characters on the authority of the Daejeon tongpyeon and Daejeon hoetong. How this bears on the comparison in Chapter 6 is set out there and on the Reliability page.

Not done in this chapter#

  • The provisions of the Gyeongje yukjeon and its supplements were not counted, as their text does not survive. Fragments quoted in the Veritable Records were not gathered and compared either.
  • The course of compilation between 1469 and 1485 is not described, as its dates were not checked article by article in the Veritable Records.
