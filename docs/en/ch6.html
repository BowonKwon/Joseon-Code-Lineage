Chapter 6. The code of 1865: four layers in the Daejeon hoetong

In short. The Daejeon hoetong (大典會通) of 1865 brought the Gyeongguk daejeon, the Sok daejeon and the Daejeon tongpyeon into one book and added later rules under the mark 補. Each layer is entered as a block headed by one of the marks 原, 續, 增 or 補, so one can count which earlier code the text of 1865 is marked as coming from. There are 3,390 marks. Of the 136,692 characters outside the office tables, text under 增 (Daejeon tongpyeon) is the largest share at 32.7%; text added in 1865 under 補 is 14.6%. Of 234 jomok, 218 have 原 (Gyeongguk daejeon) as their earliest mark.

Four codes in one book

The memorial (箋) presenting the Daejeon hoetong to the king is dated 同治四年十一月, the eleventh lunar month of 1865, and was submitted by the Chief State Councillor Jo Du-sun (趙斗淳) and others. The preface is by the Left State Councillor Kim Byeong-hak (金炳學); the text runs to six volumes. It is the last great code (大典) of Joseon in the six-jeon form, after the Gyeongguk daejeon (1485), the Sok daejeon (1746) and the Daejeon tongpyeon (1785).

According to its explanatory notes (凡例), the compilers did not, as a rule, alter the text of the three codes (they say they corrected only evident omissions and errors here and there) and printed it in order: first the Gyeongguk daejeon, marked 原; then the Sok daejeon, marked 續; then the Daejeon tongpyeon, marked 增. Provisions newly added were set apart by the character 補 printed white on black (陰刻) at their head (以補字陰刻標揭). In the 1865 print the marks appear as characters inside small boxes.

Mark Meaning Compiled
原 Gyeongguk daejeon 1485
續 Sok daejeon 1746
增 Daejeon tongpyeon 1785
補 added in the Daejeon hoetong 1865

The Jeonyul tongbo of Chapter 5 puts its marks after the text they cover; the Daejeon hoetong puts them before. The two books also mean different things by 補: royal instructions and registers in the Jeonyul tongbo, additions of 1865 in the Daejeon hoetong. This site keeps the two sets of marks apart.

Counting the marks

Terms — the six jeon (六典): the six parts into which the codes and the collections of instructions divide their text: Personnel (吏典), Revenue (戶典), Rites (禮典), Military (兵典), Punishments, i.e. the Penal Code (刑典), and Works (工典). Jomok (條目): a headed section under a jeon. Provision: a single rule under a jomok (where one ends differs by book; see Method). Sugyo (受敎): a royal instruction.

A count of marks does not depend on how far each mark reaches, so it does not change with the reading. Over the whole code, 續 from the Sok daejeon is the most frequent. Yet 78% of the marks sit in the Personnel and Military codes. Both contain office tables (京官職, 外官職 and others), and many entries in those tables carry a short note such as "reduced in 續". The explanatory notes say as much: where offices were merged or ranks changed, the old arrangement was kept and a note under 補 was added at each place (逐段以補字懸註).

Layers by characters

This chart counts the characters covered by each layer's mark. Of the 2,489 provisions, 1,188 carry no mark. Of these, 1,167 follow a marked provision in the same jomok (most begin with ○), and 21 are the document forms of the Rites Code discussed below. Since the explanatory notes say each layer is entered as a block headed by its mark, these provisions are read as carrying the mark before them (the carry reading, as in Chapter 5). To check this reading, 11 such provisions drawn at random were found in the 1865 print (a sample check; see Reliability); none of the 11 has a mark on the page either.

Office tables are left out of the main figure. A mark in a table annotates an entry, and the print does not settle whether it covers that entry alone or the entries after it. The table under the chart gives the figures with the tables and the figures counting only text that directly follows a mark (the strict reading, over all 176,496 characters including the tables).

Over the six jeon, text under 增 from the Daejeon tongpyeon is the largest share, followed by 原 from the Gyeongguk daejeon, 續 from the Sok daejeon and 補 from 1865. In the Revenue, Rites and Penal codes, 增 is 35–41%. The Works Code has the largest share of 原 (40.6%) and the smallest of 補 (4.3%).

These shares describe the sources the compilers of 1865 named. If an instruction of the Gyeongguk daejeon period first entered a code in the Sok daejeon, the provision is marked 續. A mark says in which code a provision first appeared, not the year in which it was first made.

The layer in which each jomok first appears

For each jomok (a headed section under a jeon), the earliest of its marks was taken. This is the earliest layer in time among the marks of the jomok, which may differ from the first mark on the page (the Military Code's 雜職 shows 續 first but has 原 further on, so it is counted as 原). Of 234 jomok, 218 have 原 from the Gyeongguk daejeon as their earliest mark: by the marks of the 1865 compilers, most jomok are rooted in the Gyeongguk daejeon. This does not mean that their names and content are those of 1485. Of the 15 jomok that first appear in 續, 11 are in the Penal Code (殺獄 homicide, 檢驗 inquests, 姦犯 sexual offences, 赦令 amnesties, 聽理 hearing suits and others). No jomok has 補 as its earliest mark: all 補 provisions added in 1865 sit in jomok that carry a mark of an earlier code.

At the end of the Rites Code (禮典), the first document form (文武官四品以上告身式) carries 原 and the note 下幷同 ("the same below"). The 24 forms after it (from 文武官五品以下告身式 to 準戶口式) are therefore read as 原. Of these, 21 carry no mark and 3 carry only a short note such as 增 今則… or 增 今廢.

Sources and limits

  • Text. 2,509 end entries of the table of contents (2,489 of them provisions) were counted from the Daejeon hoetong pages of the National Institute of Korean History's Korean History Database ("Legal sources of the Joseon period"). That text may be used for personal research only, so this site publishes counts, NIKH item ids and short quotations, not the text itself.
  • Checked against the print. Pages of the 1865 edition (Internet Archive, copy of the C. V. Starr East Asian Library, UC Berkeley) were compared. One mark missing from the NIKH text (續 at the head of the Military Code's 雜令) was found and added. The print divides provisions with two signs, ○ and a half-filled ◐; the NIKH text writes both as ○. The meaning of ◐ is not established and it is not used here (see Reliability).
  • Reproduced. An independent reviewer given only the counting rules recounted from the same source and obtained the same mark counts, characters per layer and jomok classification; the totals per jeon differed only by whether volume-title characters were counted (see Reliability).
  • Not done. The provisions were not aligned with the text of the three earlier codes. Separating the Gyeongguk daejeon provisions that survive unchanged to 1865 from those revised is planned for the next version (1.1).
