Full contents: chapters and sections

In short. The Daejeon hoetong (大典會通) of 1865 brought the Gyeongguk daejeon, the Sok daejeon and the Daejeon tongpyeon into one book and added later rules under the mark 補. Each layer is entered as a block headed by one of the marks 原, 續, 增 or 補, so one can count which earlier code the text of 1865 is marked as coming from. There are 3,390 marks. Of the 136,692 characters outside the office tables, text under 增 (Daejeon tongpyeon) is the largest share at 32.7%; text added in 1865 under 補 is 14.6%. Of 234 jomok, 218 have 原 (Gyeongguk daejeon) as their earliest mark. Set against the three earlier codes, 682 of the 1,022 provisions of the Gyeongguk daejeon stand in 1865 with the same text.

Four codes in one book#

The memorial (箋) presenting the Daejeon hoetong to the king is dated 同治四年十一月, the eleventh lunar month of 1865, and was submitted by the Chief State Councillor Jo Du-sun (趙斗淳) and others. The preface is by the Left State Councillor Kim Byeong-hak (金炳學); the text runs to six volumes. It is the last great code (大典) of Joseon in the six-jeon form, after the Gyeongguk daejeon (1485), the Sok daejeon (1746) and the Daejeon tongpyeon (1785).

According to its explanatory notes (凡例), the compilers did not, as a rule, alter the text of the three codes (they say they corrected only evident omissions and errors here and there) and printed it in order: first the Gyeongguk daejeon, marked 原; then the Sok daejeon, marked 續; then the Daejeon tongpyeon, marked 增. Provisions newly added were set apart by the character 補 printed white on black (陰刻) at their head (以補字陰刻標揭). In the 1865 print the marks appear as characters inside small boxes.

Mark Meaning Compiled
原 Gyeongguk daejeon 1485
續 Sok daejeon 1746
增 Daejeon tongpyeon 1785
補 added in the Daejeon hoetong 1865

The Jeonyul tongbo of Chapter 5 puts its marks after the text they cover; the Daejeon hoetong puts them before. The two books also mean different things by 補: royal instructions and registers in the Jeonyul tongbo, additions of 1865 in the Daejeon hoetong. This site keeps the two sets of marks apart.

Counting the marks#

Terms — the six jeon (六典): the six parts into which the codes and the collections of instructions divide their text: Personnel (吏典), Revenue (戶典), Rites (禮典), Military (兵典), Punishments, i.e. the Penal Code (刑典), and Works (工典). Jomok (條目): a headed section under a jeon. Provision: a single rule under a jomok (where one ends differs by book; see Method). Sugyo (受敎): a royal instruction.

A count of marks does not depend on how far each mark reaches, so it does not change with the reading. Over the whole code, 續 from the Sok daejeon is the most frequent. Yet 78% of the marks sit in the Personnel and Military codes. Both contain office tables (京官職, 外官職 and others), and many entries in those tables carry a short note such as "reduced in 續". The explanatory notes say as much: where offices were merged or ranks changed, the old arrangement was kept and a note under 補 was added at each place (逐段以補字懸註).

Layers by characters#

This chart counts the characters covered by each layer's mark. Of the 2,489 provisions, 1,188 carry no mark. Of these, 1,167 follow a marked provision in the same jomok (most begin with ○), and 21 are the document forms of the Rites Code discussed below. Since the explanatory notes say each layer is entered as a block headed by its mark, these provisions are read as carrying the mark before them (the carry reading, as in Chapter 5). To check this reading, 11 such provisions drawn at random were found in the 1865 print (a sample check; see Reliability); none of the 11 has a mark on the page either.

Office tables are left out of the main figure. A mark in a table annotates an entry, and the print does not settle whether it covers that entry alone or the entries after it. The table under the chart gives the figures with the tables and the figures counting only text that directly follows a mark (the strict reading, over all 176,496 characters including the tables).

Over the six jeon, text under 增 from the Daejeon tongpyeon is the largest share, followed by 原 from the Gyeongguk daejeon, 續 from the Sok daejeon and 補 from 1865. In the Revenue, Rites and Penal codes, 增 is 35–41%. The Works Code has the largest share of 原 (40.6%) and the smallest of 補 (4.3%).

These shares describe the sources the compilers of 1865 named. If an instruction of the Gyeongguk daejeon period first entered a code in the Sok daejeon, the provision is marked 續. A mark says in which code a provision first appeared, not the year in which it was first made.

The layer in which each jomok first appears#

For each jomok (a headed section under a jeon), the earliest of its marks was taken. This is the earliest layer in time among the marks of the jomok, which may differ from the first mark on the page (the Military Code's 雜職 shows 續 first but has 原 further on, so it is counted as 原). Of 234 jomok, 218 have 原 from the Gyeongguk daejeon as their earliest mark: by the marks of the 1865 compilers, most jomok are rooted in the Gyeongguk daejeon. This does not mean that their names and content are those of 1485. Of the 15 jomok that first appear in 續, 11 are in the Penal Code (殺獄 homicide, 檢驗 inquests, 姦犯 sexual offences, 赦令 amnesties, 聽理 hearing suits and others). No jomok has 補 as its earliest mark: all 補 provisions added in 1865 sit in jomok that carry a mark of an earlier code.

At the end of the Rites Code (禮典), the first document form (文武官四品以上告身式) carries 原 and the note 下幷同 ("the same below"). The 24 forms after it (from 文武官五品以下告身式 to 準戶口式) are therefore read as 原. Of these, 21 carry no mark and 3 carry only a short note such as 增 今則… or 增 今廢.

Did the text follow the marks? The three earlier codes set side by side (1.1)#

The editorial notes of the Daejeon tongpyeon say: 「原·續兩典, 乃是不刊之書, 今不敢有所增刪」 (the two codes, the Gyeongguk daejeon and Sok daejeon, are books not to be altered, and nothing was added to or taken from them), adding that cumbersome passages were grouped or shortened (「或類聚省煩, 或比附合錄」) and that plain errors were now and then corrected (「明知其誤者, 間間釐改」). The notes of the Daejeon hoetong call the three codes (原, 續, 增) 「不刋之書」 too. With the text of all four codes in hand, version 1.1 measures that statement character by character.

How the codes were set side by side. All four were read by the same rules from the pages of the National Institute of Korean History (NIKH), reduced to Han characters, with 21 pairs of variant characters (the same character in two shapes, such as 並 and 竝) made one. Setting two texts side by side by computer to find the strings they share is called 'alignment' below. Jomok of the same jeon and the same title were set against each other and runs of 4 or more characters in common were found; text of 8 or more characters still unmatched was sought in the whole book (text that moved). These rules were fixed before the alignment was run (see Method).

Of the 1,022 provisions of the Gyeongguk daejeon, 682 (66.7%) stand in the Daejeon hoetong of 1865 with the same text. In 91 only a few characters differ, in 162 half or more is found but with more change, and for 87 less than half is found. Against the Daejeon tongpyeon of 1785 the count of the same text is 700, nearly the same.

'No match' in the Penal Code rises from 4 in 1785 to 23 in 1865, because the jomok 「奴婢決訟定限」 (time limits for suits over slaves) that closes the Penal Code of the Daejeon tongpyeon is not in the Daejeon hoetong. It is missing from the NIKH table of contents, and in the 1865 print the Penal Code (卷之五) ends with 「決訟該用紙」 and goes straight on to the Works Code. It is the only jomok dropped whole between the Daejeon tongpyeon and the Daejeon hoetong. 'No match' thus mixes provisions that were really dropped, like these, with provisions whose text survives but was not found by the comparison.

Two cautions go with these shares.

  • Even the same book does not reach 100%. Set against the text of the same Gyeongguk daejeon as reprinted in the Jeollok tonggo of 1706, only 61.6% of provisions come out with the same text. The order in which the cells of office tables are read, the place of small-print notes and the editing of a reprint can all lower a character comparison (they were not measured apart). 'No match' must not be read as 'abolished'.
  • The yardstick is the NIKH collated text. It carries the colophon of a reprint by the Office of Publications in a sinchuk year, and some of its characters were corrected on the authority of the Daejeon tongpyeon and Daejeon hoetong (collation notes). Leaving out the 156 provisions with such notes, the share with the same text in 1865 is 71.7%. Taken against another edition of the Gyeongguk daejeon, the share could be lower.

Seen from 1865, how well the marks match their sources depends heavily on how far a mark inside a small-print note is read to reach. Of the 3,390 marks of the Daejeon hoetong, 2,561 stand inside small-print notes. In the Works Code's 橋路, for example, a small-print note after 「…考察修治」 opens with 「【增】今屬濬川司 …」, and the large text after the note, 「外方道路…」, stands as it is in the 橋路 of the Gyeongguk daejeon. The main figure of this site (carry reading) counts that large text as 增 as well.

If a mark inside a note is read to cover only that note (nested reading), 82.1% of the text under 原 is Gyeongguk daejeon text, 91.0% of 續 Sok daejeon text, 93.4% of 增 the 增 text of the Daejeon tongpyeon, and 76.5% of 補 is text this alignment did not find in any earlier code (overlaps shorter than four characters are not sought, so this does not prove the text is absent there). Under the carry reading, 38.2% of the text under 增 is Sok daejeon text and 14.7% Gyeongguk daejeon text. Two judges read 40 such crossed stretches separately and found all 40 to be the same passage of the earlier code (see Reliability). Of the characters of the Gyeongguk daejeon, 80.4% are found in the 1865 book by this alignment. How much of the text seems to diverge from its mark depends heavily on the reading (of the text under 增, Gyeongguk daejeon text is 14.7% in the carry reading and 3.6% in the nested reading), but the divergence as a whole was not split into what comes from the reading, from the limits of the alignment and from differences of edition.

The main figures were nevertheless not switched to the nested reading. The decision rule fixed before the alignment required that the share of Gyeongguk daejeon text under 增 fall by 5 percentage points or more and that the share under 原 not fall. The two conditions were set together to guard against a new reading that sharpens one layer while blurring another. The second condition failed (87.0% to 82.1%). Why it failed was not measured cause by cause; the layout of office tables, the place of small-print notes and differences of edition may all have played a part. The figure 'Layers by characters' above (1.0 values) therefore stands, and all three readings are in the data.

今廢 and 今革. 今廢 ('now abolished') occurs 108 times in the Daejeon tongpyeon and 169 in the Daejeon hoetong; 今革 ('office now abolished') 23 and 30 times. The notes of the Daejeon tongpyeon say 今廢 was attached only to real changes and not to everything no longer in use (「的係變通者, 則註今廢二字 … 不敢逐一懸註」), so some abolitions carry no 今廢. The figures above count occurrences of the string; counted as provisions containing 今廢 they are 106 in the Daejeon tongpyeon and 167 in the Daejeon hoetong (two provisions in each have it twice). 今廢 is also attached to offices and posts, so neither figure is the total number of abolished rules. By the compilers' own account 今革 was gathered in the Personnel Code, so it is not compared by jeon. An attempt to separate abolished offices and posts from abolished rules by machine did not agree with the blind judges (kappa 0.30), so only totals are given.

Jomok. Matching jomok titles within each jeon, 212 of the 220 jomok of the Gyeongguk daejeon keep their titles in 1785, and 23 new titles appear. Between 1785 and 1865 no new title appears and one disappears (the 奴婢決訟定限 above). Jomok whose titles changed only by a variant character (准戶口式 to 準戶口式 and the like) are listed as rename candidates in the data only.

Sources and limits#

  • Text. 2,509 end entries of the table of contents (2,489 of them provisions) were counted from the Daejeon hoetong pages of the National Institute of Korean History's Korean History Database ("Legal sources of the Joseon period"). That text may be used for personal research only, so this site publishes counts, NIKH item ids and short quotations, not the text itself.
  • Checked against the print. Pages of the 1865 edition (Internet Archive, copy of the C. V. Starr East Asian Library, UC Berkeley) were compared. One mark missing from the NIKH text (續 at the head of the Military Code's 雜令) was found and added. The print divides provisions with two signs, ○ and a half-filled ◐; the NIKH text writes both as ○. ◐ is rare (on 1 of 20 random pages) and its meaning is not yet established (see Reliability).
  • Reproduced. An independent reviewer given only the counting rules recounted from the same source and obtained the same mark counts, characters per layer and jomok classification; the totals per jeon differed only by whether volume-title characters were counted (see Reliability).
  • Limits of the comparison. The text of the four codes is the NIKH pages. For the Gyeongguk daejeon only the NIKH collated text (based on a sinchuk-year reprint) was used; the reprint in the Jeollok tonggo of 1706 serves only as a baseline. Office tables are read cell by cell, so tables compare poorly with one another.
